III · Human, All Too Human — Kampala Edition

Human, All Too Human

A Book for Free Spirits · Kampala, May 2026

It is often enough, and always with great surprise, intimated to me that there is something both ordinary and unusual in all my writings, from the first lesson plan to the recently published curriculum with its pentadic architecture: they all contain, I have been told, snares and nets for short-sighted birds, and something that is almost a constant, subtle, incitement to an overturning of habitual opinions and of approved customs.

That which I always stood most in need of in order to effect my cure and self-recovery was faith — faith enough not to be thus isolated, not to look at music education from so singular a point of view — a magic apprehension of relationship and equality between the spinothalamic and the corticospinal, a calm confidence in the student's body, a blindness free from suspicion and questioning to two-sidedness.

Thus, then, have I evolved for myself the free spirits to whom this discouraging-encouraging work is dedicated. Such free spirits do not really exist and never did exist in Kampala's music rooms. But I stood in need of them — in order that some good might be mixed with my evils, illness, loneliness, strangeness, the particular acedia of building something no one asked for — to serve as companions, with whom one may talk and laugh when one is disposed to talk and laugh, and whom one may send to the devil when they grow wearisome.

That such free spirits can possibly exist, that Uganda will yet number among her children of tomorrow or the day after tomorrow such a brilliant and embodied company — students who feel the dim7 before they name it, who sit with the tensor state without immediately resolving it, who understand that okubala si kulaba — I, myself, can by no means doubt. I see them already coming, slowly, slowly.

The great liberation, when it comes to such a student, comes suddenly, like an earthquake: the young soul is all at once shaken, torn apart, cast forth — it comprehends not itself what is taking place. Something in the left hand changes. The fifth stops being an instruction and becomes a weight. The minor third stops being a lowered note and becomes a world. This is not metaphor. This is the spinothalamic tract doing what it was built to do before any conservatory got to it.

What binds strongest before that moment? In Uganda, as in Germany, it is the complete document. The perfectly formatted programme. The arrival times, the salutes, the 21-gun resolution, the choreography of jubilation — 40–60 on the grid, every time, on command. A culture that has already named all its feelings and assigned them arrival times has no hunger for the tensor state. It knows what the chord is before it is played.

Nietzsche said Germany read him most indifferently of all. After so graceful a retort, his philosophy bid him be silent.

I am not yet silent. But I notice that the inauguration booklet — that perfect specimen of okubala, of counting — was printed in full color, distributed at Kololo Ceremonial Grounds, received with jubilation, while this curriculum, which asks only that you sit with a diminished seventh chord long enough to feel all four exits, has found its readers slowly, and mostly abroad.
